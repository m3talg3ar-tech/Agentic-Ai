Section 1 — Kirkpatrick Blueprint

Designed Backwards: From Results to Reaction

The Kirkpatrick Model is applied in reverse order during design — starting with the organizational outcome we want to support, then working back to the learner experience. This ensures every instructional decision is anchored to real impact.

Level 4 — Results (Organizational / Societal Outcome)

Goal
Students become informed citizens and emerging professionals who can critically engage with AI governance questions — reducing the risk of ungoverned AI deployment in their future workplaces and contributing to stronger public policy discourse.

Level 3 — Behavior (Transfer to Real-World Actions)

After completing this lesson, students will demonstrate the following behaviors in academic and professional contexts:

  • Critically evaluate agentic AI tools before adoption, asking governance questions about accountability, transparency, and scope
  • Apply a policy lens when discussing or writing about AI in coursework, capstone projects, and internship settings
  • Raise governance considerations proactively in team discussions or civic conversations involving AI systems

Level 2 — Learning (Knowledge, Skills & Attitudes)

Learning TypeOutcome
KnowledgeDefine agentic AI and distinguish it from traditional AI and automation
KnowledgeIdentify the core governance gaps: accountability, transparency, and auditability
KnowledgeName at least two real-world policy frameworks relevant to AI (e.g., EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF)
SkillAnalyze a given AI system scenario using a governance checklist lens
SkillFormulate a policy recommendation supported by a reasoned argument
AttitudeAppreciate the urgency of AI oversight before capabilities outpace regulation

Level 1 — Reaction (Engagement & Relevance)

The lesson is designed to feel urgent and personally relevant — not abstract. Engagement strategies include:

  • Opening with a real-world agentic AI incident as the hook (creates immediate relevance)
  • A structured debate moment that gives students a voice and surfaces prior beliefs
  • Explicit connection to career paths: security analyst, policy advisor, compliance officer, AI auditor
  • eLearning interactivity (scenario branching) that makes the stakes feel real

Section 3 — Lesson Outline & Instructional Flow

15–30 Minute Blended Lesson Structure

The lesson is split across two modalities. eLearning delivers foundational content and scenario interaction; the instructor-led segment drives discussion, application, and reflection. Facilitators may expand any phase for the 30-minute version.

PhaseTimeModalityActivityK-Level
Hook0–3 mineLearningLearner watches a 60-second real incident clip: an autonomous AI agent approving financial transactions without human review. Prompt: 'Who is responsible when this goes wrong?'L1 — Reaction
Concept3–10 mineLearningInteractive slides define agentic AI, contrast it with traditional AI, and introduce the three governance gaps: accountability, transparency, and auditability. Includes a 3-question knowledge check.L2 — Knowledge
Scenario10–16 mineLearningBranching scenario: 'The Hiring Agent.' Students play a compliance officer who must decide whether to approve an agentic AI hiring tool. Three decision points, each with governance consequences revealed.L2 — Skill
Debrief16–22 minInstructor-ledInstructor facilitates a structured discussion using the Governance Lens Protocol (see Section 5). Small groups defend their scenario decisions. Dissenting views are surfaced and examined.L2–L3
Apply22–28 minInstructor-ledPairs complete the 'Policy Memo Sprint': a 5-minute structured template asking students to identify one governance gap and propose one policy lever to address it.L3 — Behavior
Close28–30 minInstructor-ledInstructor connects lesson to career paths and emerging frameworks (EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF). Students share one 'governance question they'll carry forward.'L4 — Results

Section 4 — Core Case Study: The Hiring Agent

Scenario Overview

This scenario is the instructional centerpiece of the lesson. It is designed to be morally ambiguous — reasonable people can disagree — which drives productive governance debate.

Setting
Year: 2027. A mid-sized financial services firm, Arbor Capital, has deployed 'ArcHire' — a third-party agentic AI system that autonomously screens, interviews (via chat), scores, and advances candidates through the first two rounds of hiring with no human review until the final interview stage.
Trigger
A rejected candidate, Danielle Torres, files a complaint. She scored in the 97th percentile on technical assessments but was filtered out by ArcHire. The system's decision log shows only: 'Cultural alignment score: 41/100.' No further explanation is provided. Arbor Capital's legal team cannot explain what 'cultural alignment' means or how it is computed.

Decision Points in the Branching Scenario

1. Should Arbor Capital be required to disclose that ArcHire, not a human, made the screening decision?

  • Policy lever: Transparency & disclosure requirements
  • Governance gap: Candidates have no right to know an AI rejected them

2. Who is accountable for the 'cultural alignment' score — Arbor Capital, the ArcHire vendor, or both?

  • Policy lever: Liability assignment in AI procurement contracts
  • Governance gap: Diffused accountability across AI supply chains

3. Should ArcHire be suspended pending a bias audit, even if Arbor Capital has no legal obligation to do so?

  • Policy lever: Voluntary governance standards vs. mandatory regulation
  • Governance gap: Absence of pre-deployment audit requirements
Note
There is no 'right' answer in this scenario. The goal is for students to articulate which governance principle is most at stake in each decision point, and to connect their choice to a real or proposed policy instrument. Disagreement is a feature, not a problem.

Section 5 — Assessment & Evaluation

Measuring Learning Across Kirkpatrick Levels

Level 1 — Reaction Pulse (Immediate)

At the end of the eLearning module, learners rate the following on a 5-point scale. Benchmark target: average score of 4.0 or above.

  • This lesson felt relevant to my future career.
  • The scenario made me think differently about AI.
  • I would recommend this lesson to a classmate.

Level 2 — Knowledge Check (eLearning, Auto-Graded)

Q1 — Maps to LO1, LO2
ArcHire autonomously schedules interviews and advances candidates. Which characteristic makes this an agentic AI system rather than a traditional decision-support tool?

A) It uses a large language model    B) It takes sequential actions toward a goal without human approval at each step
C) It stores candidate data    D) It was built by a third-party vendor    Correct: B
Q2 — Maps to LO3
Arbor Capital cannot explain how ArcHire's 'cultural alignment' score is calculated. Which governance gap does this represent?

A) Accountability    B) Scalability    C) Auditability    D) Interoperability    Correct: C
Q3 — Maps to LO4
Which action best demonstrates applying a governance lens to an agentic AI system?

A) Testing for speed    B) Asking 'who is responsible if this system causes harm and how would we know?'
C) Checking vendor reputation    D) Verifying HTTPS    Correct: B

Level 2–3 — Policy Memo Sprint

Pairs complete this structured 5-minute template. Formative — used for discussion, not a grade.

To:A regulator, an executive, or a standards body (student chooses)
Re:One governance gap identified in the ArcHire scenario
The problem:Describe the gap in 2–3 sentences. What harm could it cause?
Our recommendation:Propose one specific policy lever or standard to address it
Why it matters:Connect to a broader principle: accountability, transparency, or auditability

Level 3 — Transfer Evidence (Follow-Up, 2–4 Weeks Later)

  • Since this lesson, I raised a governance or policy question about an AI tool in class or at work.
  • I applied a governance lens when reading or discussing a news story about AI.
  • I feel more confident asking 'who is accountable?' when evaluating an AI system.

Section 6 — Facilitator Notes

Running the Instructor-Led Segment

Governance Lens Protocol — Debrief Discussion

Use these four questions to structure the 6-minute group debrief. Write them on screen or a whiteboard:

Q1
Who is accountable if ArcHire harms a candidate — and how would you prove it?
Q2
What information would a regulator need to audit this system, and does it exist?
Q3
Which decision in the scenario made you most uncomfortable — and why?
Q4
What one policy rule, if it existed today, would have prevented this problem?

Facilitation Tips

  • Do not resolve the disagreement. The goal is articulating a governance-grounded position, not consensus.
  • If discussion stalls: ask 'What would a regulator say?' or 'What would the candidate's lawyer argue?'
  • For 15-minute version: skip the Policy Memo Sprint; use only the debrief questions.
  • For 30-minute version: allow each pair to share their memo recommendation (1 min each) before the close.

Connecting to Policy Frameworks

FrameworkRelevance to This Lesson
EU AI Act (2024)Classifies AI systems by risk level; high-risk systems (e.g., hiring AI) require transparency, human oversight, and documentation.
NIST AI Risk Management FrameworkProvides a voluntary framework for governing AI across trustworthiness dimensions: accountable, transparent, explainable, privacy-enhanced, secure.
OECD AI PrinciplesInternational baseline principles including transparency, accountability, and robustness — useful for cross-border policy discussions.

Differentiation Options

  • Advanced students: assign a 1-page policy brief comparing EU AI Act requirements to the ArcHire scenario as homework.
  • Less experienced students: provide a pre-filled governance checklist before the scenario to scaffold their analysis.
  • Online/async delivery: replace the instructor-led debrief with a structured discussion board prompt using the same four Governance Lens questions.

Section 7 — Design Rationale & Next Steps

Why This Lesson Is Designed This Way

Why Backwards Design
Starting from Level 4 (societal outcomes) ensures that every instructional element — the scenario, the objectives, the memo sprint — is anchored to something real. Without this anchor, short lessons often optimize for engagement at the expense of transfer.
Why a Hiring Scenario
Hiring AI sits at the intersection of law, ethics, and technology — three domains undergraduates can reason about without deep technical knowledge. It is also a domain where real incidents have occurred (Amazon's 2018 resume screening tool, HireVue lawsuits), making it credible and researchable. Students can see themselves as the candidate, the compliance officer, or the regulator — which drives personal engagement.
Why Policy Memo Format
The memo format is the real-world artifact of governance work. By writing even a 5-sentence version, students experience what policy thinking feels like — not just what it means. This is the bridge between Level 2 learning and Level 3 behavioral transfer.
Why No 'Right Answer'
Governance is genuinely contested. Reasonable experts disagree about where liability sits in AI supply chains, how much transparency is sufficient, and when regulation should lead versus follow innovation. A lesson that presents one correct answer would be intellectually dishonest and would not prepare students to navigate real ambiguity.

Suggested Next Steps for Curriculum Development

  • Develop a companion eLearning storyboard for the branching scenario (3 decision branches × 2 paths each)
  • Create a facilitator slide deck with embedded discussion timers and the Governance Lens Protocol
  • Design a Level 3 follow-up survey instrument and data collection process
  • Pilot with one section; collect Level 1 reaction data and iterate before broader rollout
  • Consider a follow-on lesson on technical controls (audit logging, model cards, human-in-the-loop design) to pair with this governance introduction
