S-07  ·  Decision 2 of 3  ·  Accountability
"Arbor Capital procured ArcHire. The vendor built and trained it. Neither party can explain the 'cultural alignment' score. When Danielle was harmed — who is responsible?"
Option A  ·  Shared Accountability
Both Arbor Capital and the ArcHire vendor share joint responsibility
Each party is accountable within their sphere of control — the vendor for building an opaque system, Arbor Capital for choosing to deploy it in a high-stakes context without being able to explain it.
Option B  ·  Vendor Liability
The ArcHire vendor is solely responsible — Arbor Capital just used the tool
Arbor Capital deployed ArcHire as designed. They did not build the algorithm, set the scoring criteria, or create the cultural alignment metric. The vendor should bear the liability.
✓  Governance Principle: Accountability
Exactly right. Accountability in AI supply chains is shared — and deliberately so. Arbor Capital chose to deploy a system whose decision logic it couldn't explain. That's a governance failure on their part. The vendor built a system producing opaque, potentially discriminatory scores. That's theirs. The EU AI Act places obligations on both deployers and providers — because when harm diffuses across a supply chain, assigning it to one party lets the other off the hook entirely.
📋  Policy Connection: NIST AI RMF — organizations must govern AI across procurement, deployment, and operation. EU AI Act — "providers" and "deployers" carry distinct but overlapping legal obligations throughout the AI lifecycle.
⚠  Governance Gap Exposed: Accountability
This argument is tempting — and it's the one most organizations instinctively make. But courts, regulators, and victims rarely accept it. When Arbor Capital chose to use ArcHire in a consequential hiring context, it took on responsibility for what the system does. "We didn't build it" is not a governance strategy — it's how AI harms go unaddressed. Diffused accountability, where every party points at another, is one of the central risks of agentic AI at scale.
⚠️  Consider: Several US state laws hold employers — not AI vendors — liable for discriminatory automated hiring decisions. New York City's Local Law 144 explicitly targets the deployer, not the developer.

S-10  ·  Decision 3 of 3  ·  Auditability
"Your legal team confirms: no obligation to suspend ArcHire while the complaint is investigated. But no one at Arbor Capital can explain what ArcHire decided or why. It's still running. What do you do?"
Option A  ·  Governance-Aware
Suspend ArcHire immediately and commission an independent algorithmic audit
Pause all ArcHire screening until an independent third party can audit its decision logic, test for bias, and confirm whether its outputs are fair and explainable — before resuming.
Option B  ·  Business Continuity
Continue using ArcHire — there is no legal requirement to stop
Arbor Capital has no legal obligation to suspend operations. Halting hiring mid-cycle would create significant business disruption and may signal guilt to regulators before any determination of wrongdoing.
✓  Governance Principle: Auditability
A principled choice — and the hardest one, because it has a real business cost. Auditability means being able to examine what an AI system did, why it did it, and whether it was fair. Without that ability, you are operating a decision-maker you cannot hold to account. Suspending ArcHire isn't about admitting guilt — it's about recognising that you should not continue making consequential decisions with a system you cannot explain.
📋  Policy Connection: NIST AI RMF — "accountable" AI requires documentation and traceability across the system lifecycle. EU AI Act requires conformity assessments and technical documentation for high-risk AI before and during deployment.
⚠  Governance Gap Exposed: Auditability
Legal compliance and good governance are not the same thing. Every candidate ArcHire screens this week may be subject to the same flawed, unexplainable scoring. The harm compounds — and the audit trail grows harder to reconstruct with every passing day. When regulators examine this case, the fact that Arbor Capital continued operating a system it could not explain will be very difficult to defend.
⚠️  Consider: NYC Local Law 144 requires bias audits for automated employment decision tools before use. Operating without audit capability is precisely the gap this law was written to close. This decision will be a central topic in your class debrief.
